More and more companies are moving new-hire onboarding, product knowledge, partner enablement, and compliance training online. Yet many training livestreams end up as "we went live, nobody listened, and nobody remembered anything." Below is a complete training livestream workflow, laid out along the timeline.

> A training livestream isn't an in-person class moved in front of a camera. It's a complete process: design, delivery, capture, and evaluation.

## 1. Before the session: design and preparation

### 1. Define training goals and audience

Start by answering three questions: **who is learning, what should they be able to do afterward, and how will you measure it?** For example, "within two weeks, frontline sales reps can independently present the three key selling points of the new product and pass an in-session quiz" is much easier to design and evaluate than "run new-product training."

### 2. Choose the right live format

| Format | Best for | Interaction |
|---|---|---|
| Single instructor lecture | Policy briefings, rules explained | Q&A panel, polls |
| Instructor + guest conversation | Experience sharing, case reviews | Co-streaming, audience questions |
| Small interactive group | Skills practice, role-play | Multi-party co-streaming, whiteboard |
| Series bootcamp | Structured curricula | Check-ins, assignments, quizzes |

### 3. Registration and notifications

- Push the registration link through WeCom, email, or your intranet portal, and set viewing permissions so only employees or a named list can get in.
- Send reminders the day before and 15 minutes before the start.
- Publish the agenda and pre-reading in advance so learners arrive with questions.

### 4. Equipment and network rehearsal

- Use a wired connection on the streaming computer and keep a backup (such as a 4G/5G hotspot).
- The microphone matters more than the camera: clear sound beats an HD picture.
- Run at least one full rehearsal a day ahead, checking slide sharing, co-streaming, and interaction features.
- Assign clear roles: instructor, host/teaching assistant, technical support, and Q&A moderator.

## 2. During the session: interaction and facilitation

### 1. Use interaction points to fight attention drop-off

Schedule an interaction every 10–15 minutes, for example:

- **Opening poll**: gauge learners' starting level and test the interaction tools at the same time.
- **In-session quiz**: check each topic with 2–3 questions right after you cover it.
- **Case discussion**: have learners post their approach in chat, and the instructor picks a few to comment on.
- **Co-streamed questions**: invite learners to join on mic to make it feel more live.

### 2. The teaching assistant's role

The teaching assistant collects questions from chat, handles technical help requests, moderates inappropriate messages, and passes the most frequent questions to the instructor at the right moment. A good assistant lets the instructor stay focused on the content.

### 3. Contingency plans

Agree in advance on what happens if the stream drops, the instructor disconnects, or slides won't play—for example, the assistant takes over the live room to explain, or you switch to a backup streaming device.

## 3. After the session: capture and closing the loop

### 1. Turn live into on-demand

Edit the replay into short videos by topic, add chapter markers and captions, and put them in your knowledge base or learning platform so employees can find them when they need them. Many platforms can now automatically generate captions, chapter outlines, and summaries, which greatly reduces post-production work.

### 2. Quizzes and assignments

Assign a post-session quiz or practical task to turn "I heard it" into "I can use it." Employees who missed the livestream can be required to watch the replay and complete the quiz by a deadline.

### 3. Data review

Track these categories of data:

- attendance rate, average watch time, completion rate;
- interaction participation, number of questions, and question types;
- quiz accuracy and per-question error distribution (which shows which topics weren't explained clearly);
- learner satisfaction scores and suggestions.

### 4. Build a course asset catalog

As sessions add up, catalog and tag courses by role, topic, and difficulty, and clean out outdated content regularly. New hires can follow role-based learning paths, experienced staff can search on demand, and the training team can see clearly which topics are covered and which are missing.

## 4. Evaluating impact with the Kirkpatrick model

The Kirkpatrick Model is a widely used framework for evaluating training:

1. **Reaction**: were learners satisfied, and did they find it useful? — post-session survey.
2. **Learning**: did they acquire the knowledge and skills? — in-session quizzes and exams.
3. **Behavior**: are they applying it at work? — manager observation, spot checks.
4. **Results**: did it improve the business? — for example, changes in sales, complaint rates, or compliance incidents.

The advantage of live online training is that data for the first two levels can be collected automatically. The last two require working with business units to keep tracking for a period after training.

## 5. Summary

A training livestream isn't an in-person class moved in front of a camera; it's a complete process of design, delivery, capture, and evaluation. When choosing a platform, use this workflow to check for access control, interaction tools, encrypted replays, and learning data. Several providers already offer dedicated training livestream solutions—shortlist a few against your own process and compare them in a trial.
