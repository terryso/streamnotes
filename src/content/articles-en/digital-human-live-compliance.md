Digital-human hosts, AI co-hosts, and virtual-avatar selling have moved from demos into scalable operations. Regulators have moved with them—from high-level principles to enforceable labeling and liability rules. The *Measures for the Labeling of AI-Generated and Synthesized Content*, issued in March 2025 and effective from 1 September 2025, together with the *Measures for the Supervision and Administration of Livestream E-Commerce* effective from 1 February 2026, tighten three lines at once: authorization, labeling, and content review. For enterprises, compliance is no longer a one-time legal sign-off before launch; it is part of daily livestream operations.

> Digital-human livestream compliance fits in three sentences: secure the rights first, apply the right labels, then review scripts and products as strictly as you would for a human host.

## 1. Why digital-human livestream compliance got stricter

Over the past few years, deep-synthesis and generative-AI rules arrived in sequence. The deep-synthesis provisions (in force since 2023) require separate consent for editing face, voice, and other biometrics, and prominent labels when generated content may confuse the public. The interim generative-AI measures govern services offered to the public. For enterprise livestreams, the most visible shifts are:

1. **Labeling moved from “some notice” to explicit + implicit dual tracks.** The labeling measures define both explicit labels (clearly perceptible text, audio, or graphics) and implicit labels (technical marks in file metadata), with placement rules for text, audio, images, video, and virtual scenes. Distribution platforms must check metadata and prompt user declarations. No organization or individual may maliciously delete, alter, forge, or conceal required labels.
2. **Livestream e-commerce wrote digital-human hosts into liability clauses.** Operators that use AI-generated person images or videos for livestream e-commerce must label them as required and keep prompting consumers; if violations occur, the livestream-room operator that manages or uses the avatar bears responsibility.

Earlier *Measures for the Administration of Livestream Marketing (Trial)* already required platforms to assess and prominently label virtual images built with AI, digital vision, VR, or speech synthesis, and to obtain consent when using another person’s likeness as a virtual selling avatar (with voice protected by reference). Medical teaching streams and financial private-domain livestreams already carry sector rules; adding a digital human lengthens the review chain rather than shortening it. See also this site’s notes on [medical conference and surgery livestreams](/en/articles/medical-conference-surgery-live/) and [bank and securities private-domain livestreams](/en/articles/bank-securities-private-live/).

## 2. Authorization: likeness, voice, and avatar ownership

Handle digital-human rights on three tracks instead of one vague “image license”:

| Type | Key requirements | Common gaps |
|---|---|---|
| Human-driven / voice or face clone | Likeness and voice rights; for face/voice biometric editing, inform the person and obtain **separate consent** | Voice and re-training omitted; term, territory, and channels unclear |
| Original virtual avatar | Copyright, trademark, and commercial scope; ownership of models, assets, and derivatives | Vendor keeps re-license rights; employee-created work ownership unclear |
| Third-party assets and voice libraries | Verify commercial license and banned uses; keep invoices and version IDs | Using “personal learning only” voices or mocap data |

The contract chain should connect the rights holder, the digital-human vendor, the livestream-room operator / brand, and any MCN. Spell out real-time driving, VOD re-edits, ads, cross-border use, and destruction duties after takedown. Archive authorization files, version changes, and retirement records so you can prove compliance later.

## 3. Labeling: explicit, implicit, and ongoing prompts

A corner badge is not enough. Implement both production-side and distribution-side controls.

**Explicit labels (clearly perceptible):** place prominent prompts at the start of video and around the player, with optional mid-stream prompts; for virtual scenes, prompt at the opening frame and keep prompts during continuous service; for audio, add voice or rhythm cues at start, end, or mid points. Downloads, copies, and exports must still contain compliant explicit labels.

**Implicit labels (metadata and similar):** write generation attributes, provider name or code, and content IDs into file metadata; digital watermarks are encouraged. When distributors detect implicit labels, user declarations, or other generation traces, they must add prominent notices and append distribution metadata.

Livestream e-commerce further requires **ongoing prompts**—not a one-frame flash at go-live. Keep a consumer-visible notice that the person image or video is AI-generated. Mandatory national standard [GB 45438-2025](https://openstd.samr.gov.cn/bzgk/std/newGbInfo?hcno=F32EA2A561F1886CD8D606513512D547) took effect with the labeling measures and is a practical engineering baseline. Malicious removal, alteration, forgery, or concealment of required labels—and tools that enable those acts—are prohibited. For how AI enters live and VOD pipelines more broadly, see [How AI is changing live and VOD](/en/articles/ai-in-live-vod/).

## 4. Content review: scripts, advertising law, and accountable parties

An AI host does not create a liability shield. Script review, product verification, advertising compliance, and platform rules apply at least as strictly as for human hosts; 24/7 operation usually needs stronger automation plus human escalation.

Watch these risks closely:

1. **False or misleading commercial promotion.** The livestream e-commerce rules bar using AI to fabricate or spread false or misleading commercial information, or to impersonate others in promotion.
2. **Advertising-role duties.** When livestream content constitutes a commercial ad, fulfill publisher, agent, or endorser duties as applicable; clipped replays used in paid placement can also fall under advertising rules.
3. **Platform vs. room-operator split.** Platforms must assess and prominently label virtual-image marketing and keep inspection and high-risk enforcement capacity; room operators own product selection, narration, on-the-spot correction, and interaction moderation. Anyone actually operating another party’s room owes the same operator duties.
4. **Sector overlays.** Healthcare, finance, and education still need qualification display, banned-goods controls, and any filing duties; when choosing a livestream platform, include compliance capability in the scorecard—see [enterprise livestream platform selection](/en/articles/enterprise-live-platform-selection/).

At minimum: pre-stream script and asset review, in-stream sensitive-word and banned-goods interception, and post-stream replay sampling with retained logs. AI is a first-pass filter; edge cases still need humans.

## 5. Enterprise checklist

**Before go-live:** separate consents for likeness/voice/biometrics; clear avatar ownership; liability and indemnity in vendor and ops contracts; explicit-label design and implicit-label export path reviewed; script library, banned lists, and credentials ready; filing or safety-assessment materials verifiable where required.

**During the stream:** keep a persistent AI-generation prompt; do not present covers or titles as a live human host if they are not; keep real-time review and human patrol online with on-the-spot correction; control comments, outbound links, and product pages together.

**After the stream:** keep labels on replays and paid clips; stop use and delete or seal data when rights expire or the avatar is retired; retain logs, review records, and transaction-related information per law and platform rules.

## 6. Takeaway

Digital humans lower the cost of putting a face on camera; they do not lower the cost of compliance. Split authorization, labeling, and content review into operable controls, and write accountability into operator and vendor contracts—cheaper than waiting for traffic limits or regulatory interviews. Rules will keep refining; version your internal checklist as CAC, market-regulation, and national-standard guidance updates.
